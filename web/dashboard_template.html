What can this building become, and what does becoming it cost?

A computational lab for adaptive reuse. One 1980s Stockholm office shell is tested against six use programmes and fourteen user groups, over fifty years, in SEK and kg CO2e. Flexibility here is not an adjective. It is the measured cost of moving from one use to the next.

Stage 01 · Retain

What already exists

The shell is the constraint every scenario is designed within, and the carbon already spent on it cannot be unspent. Demolition forfeits that investment and spends new carbon to replace it — which is the strongest argument reuse has.

Blocking unknowns

These change conclusions, not just precision. Every result below is conditional on them, and the engine says so rather than hiding it.

Stage 03 · Adapt

Which uses does the shell allow?

Each programme is tested against the shell and reports what stops it first. Capacity is given twice: on the existing services, and once services are renewed — they have five years of life left and are being replaced regardless, so treating today's plant as a permanent ceiling would understate every option. The gap between the two bars is the services investment.

Stage 03 · Adapt

Parametric layout on the structural grid

The unit of allocation is the structural bay, not the room, because in reuse the grid is fixed and the grid decides everything. Zones that need daylight compete for perimeter bays; the rest are pushed deep. The output that matters is not the plan — it is the partition quantity, which becomes cost and carbon.

White inner edge = bay touches the facade. Dark cell = core.

Stage 03 · Adapt · Rhino / Grasshopper

The building model, floor by floor

The bay engine above answers how much wall a use needs. This is the same question asked of a real footprint drawn in Rhino: every wall, door and window is an element with an id, a catalogue component and a state. The existing facade, core and slabs are retained and carry no new carbon; everything drawn in colour is new fit-out. Grasshopper exports the bill of quantities and the engine prices it again with the same catalogue and method as every other figure on this page.

Stage 05 · Evaluate

The third KPI

Cost and carbon total easily and therefore dominate. Daylight, quiet, a view from a seated position, whether staff can work without injuring themselves — these have no natural unit and vanish from comparisons unless something forces them in. Scores are shown per dimension and never only as a blend, because weighting daylight against acoustics is a value judgement, not a measurement.

Conflicting objectives

Recorded, not resolved. Where two objectives genuinely oppose each other, silently optimising one away is how a model launders a design decision into a technical result.

Stage 05 · Evaluate

Fifty years, five futures

Each timeline is a sequence of uses. Carbon is split into what is spent before anyone moves in, what each change of use costs, and what the building emits while running. Module D — the reuse credit — is reported but never netted into any total, per EN 15978. Carbon is not discounted; money is.

The reversibility–carbon trade-off

Every component carries a reversibility score — what fraction can be detached without destroying it — alongside its product-stage carbon. Low-carbon and reusable are different properties, and the chart below is the argument for keeping them apart: the cheapest carbon is often the least recoverable.

Onboarding

What we need from suppliers

One JSON object per product. Most EPDs stop at A1–A3; the three fields below that suppliers usually cannot fill are the three that decide whether a reuse argument holds. Expect the request itself to be productive — it separates suppliers who have thought about disassembly from those who have thought about marketing.
